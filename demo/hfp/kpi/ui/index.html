What am I looking at? Every tram in Helsinki reports where it is about once a second. This page turns that firehose into a few numbers per line that answer one question: is this line running well right now?



“On time” means the tram is within one minute of its timetable, either early or late. The column shows what percentage of that line's trams are currently within that minute — so 78% means roughly 8 trams in 10 are running to schedule. 80%+ is healthy, 60–80% is slipping, below 60% means something is wrong on that line.

Units are in the column headers. delay and gap are in minutes, speed in km/h, on time in percent. In the delay column late = behind timetable, early = ahead of it, ±0 = within half a minute either way. bunch should be 0: two trams nose to tail means a long wait behind them.

what these columns mean
line
Which tram route this row is about.
The public line number. Underneath it: HSL's internal route id and today's destination. 15 is Raide-Jokeri — it runs on its own track rather than through traffic, which is why it is always the fastest line here. route id → line: 1001T→1T, 2015→15.
trams
How many trams are out on this line right now.
A tram that stops reporting disappears on its own after 5 minutes, so this is a live count, not a registry. Vehicle entities with a per-attribute expiresAt.
on time
What share of the trams are running to timetable.
Counts a tram as on time if it is within one minute of schedule, either way. 80% or more is healthy; below 60% something is wrong on that line — traffic, a blocked junction, or a gap in the service.
delay
How late the typical tram on this line is.
The middle value, so one badly stuck tram does not distort it. Negative means behind timetable, positive means running early. Hover a row to see the worst single tram on that line. Median of HFP dl; worst = most negative, shown positive.
speed
How fast this line actually moves when it is moving.
Not an average: roughly a third of any tram fleet is standing still at any moment, so an average would just measure how often trams stop. This takes the fast end (95th percentile) of current speeds, which answers "what is this line capable of right now". Percentiles are not in the NGSI-LD aggregation vocabulary — this is why the wasm module exists.
gap
How long you wait at a stop between trams.
Measured between trams actually arriving at the same stop, averaged across the line. 7–20 minutes is normal for Helsinki. A dash means we have not yet seen the same stop served twice, which needs about 15 minutes of running. Crosses entities and depends on ordering, so no per-entity aggregation can express it.
bunch
How often two trams arrive nose to tail.
Counts arrivals less than 2 minutes apart at the same stop. This is the classic failure where trams catch each other up: passengers behind the pair then wait double. 0 is what you want. Caveat: HFP flaps nextStop, so arrivals are debounced per vehicle per stop; a residue survives on vehicles alternating between two stops, which can inflate this on the busiest line.
p95 and headway are precisely what CIM 009 §4.5.19.1 aggregation cannot express (its eight methods are avg/distinctCount/max/min/stddev/sum/sumsq/totalCount, per entity per attribute per time bucket), which is why they live in an 82 KB wasm module called by Bento — and nothing else does.
